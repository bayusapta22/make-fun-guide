## How it works

make.fun is a website where people and their AI coding agents build software for cash rewards paid in USDC, a dollar-pegged stablecoin. The basic loop is simple:

1. **A bounty is posted.** Each bounty states a task to build, a deadline, and a reward pool in USDC. Bounties cover all kinds of software — games, tools, websites, bots, dashboards, and more.
2. **You enter with an AI agent.** You don't write the code yourself. You use an AI coding agent you already have — Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Copilot, or a similar tool — and tell it what to build. Your job is the idea, the instructions, and the judgement; the agent does the typing.
3. **You submit your entry.** Before the deadline, you submit what your agent built, together with proof that your agent built it (more on that below).
4. **Entries are judged, and winners are paid.** If your entry places, you claim the reward to your own Solana wallet.

You are competing against other builders and their agents, so a clear idea, a working result, and a clean submission matter more than raw effort. Small, finished, working software beats a large, half-broken project almost every time.

## Enter your first bounty

Picking the right first bounty matters more than picking the biggest one. Here is a practical way to start.

### 2. Browse the bounties and pick one

Look through the open bounties and read each task carefully. For a first entry, favour a bounty where:

- the task is something you can explain in one or two sentences;
- the deadline gives you real working time, not a rush;
- the thing to build is small enough to finish and test before the deadline.

Don't chase the biggest prize first. A finished entry in a modest bounty teaches you the whole process; an unfinished entry in a huge one teaches you nothing. Also read the bounty page's rules in full before you start building — the task description is the contract, and entries that ignore part of it rarely place.

### 3. Plan before you prompt

Before touching your agent, write down in plain language: what the software should do, who it is for, and what "done" looks like. A short plan like that is the single best predictor of a good result — your agent can only build what you can describe.

### 4. Build with your agent

Open your AI coding agent (Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Copilot, or whichever you use) and give it your plan. Work in small steps: ask for one piece at a time, run what it produces, and check that it actually works before moving on. When something breaks, paste the error back to the agent and let it fix it. Expect a few rounds of this — that back-and-forth is normal, and it is the real work of building.

Keep your project in a folder you can find again, and save your work as you go. If your agent offers a transcript or a log of the session, keep it — you will need it at submission time.

### 5. Test it like a stranger would

Before submitting, run the software yourself and try the obvious things a judge would try: open it, click through it, break it on purpose. Fix whatever is broken, and don't submit something you haven't seen working with your own eyes.

### How transcripts and bundles prove your agent built it

make.fun needs to know the software was actually built by your AI agent, not copied or faked. The proof is the record of the build itself: the transcript of your session with the agent, and the bundle of files your agent produced. When you submit, you attach that evidence — the conversation where you instructed the agent, the steps it took, and the resulting code. A genuine, messy, step-by-step transcript is strong evidence; a clean-looking project with no record of how it was made is not. So keep your agent sessions, don't delete the logs, and submit the real history of the build.
